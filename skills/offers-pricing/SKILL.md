# Offers and pricing: earn more per customer before buying more customers

For the profitability of one proposed price, discount or ad-funded offer, use `offer-profitability` first. Use this guide for broader positioning, packaging and price-test design once those economics are understood.

## How pricing actually makes money

- **Price is the biggest lever and the least used.** A price rise lands on every future sale with no extra ad spend. At 40% contribution margin a 10% raise survives losing 20% of customers; a 10% discount needs 33% more units just to stand still.
- **Most small businesses are underpriced, not overpriced.** 65% of US home-service businesses raised prices in the prior year, 80% of those above $500K (Jobber, Dec 2025). Operators who raised almost never report losing more than the break-even share; the common regret is waiting years.
- **Raises lose the worst customers first.** A social media manager raised 22 clients 40%: 7 left, revenue +12%, work -35%, and the leavers were the most revision-heavy (r/smallbusiness, 1,239 upvotes).
- **The money hides after the yes.** Optional quote lines are accepted 25 to 50% of the time (Jobber 2026); post-purchase offers lift session value 18% at the median, 33% top quartile (Funnelish 2026). Only 16% of home-service operators quote good-better-best.
- **Discounts train customers.** One 20% coupon raised expectations of future discounts in a randomized retailer test, with modeled long-run profit loss above 10% (2023). Value-adds do not reset the reference price.
- **Risk, not price, is usually the objection.** Fix proof, guarantee and time-to-result before cutting price.

## First session: ask, then compute

Ask the owner:

1. What they sell, to whom, in which country; solo or team; capacity (hours, slots, stock).
2. Price list or recent quotes, with what is included; date of the last increase.
3. Variable costs per unit or job: materials, labor, travel, fees, shipping, refunds. Processor and marketplace statements.
4. Close rate on quotes or conversion rate; repeat rate or churn; refunds and redos.
5. Every live discount, code, friend rate, legacy price and who has it.
6. How full the calendar is, or how fast stock turns.
7. What competitors charge and what the customer would do instead of buying.
8. Offers they will never run (discounts, guarantees, payment plans); who approves price changes.
9. Public promises already made (lifetime prices, founding rates, contracts with fixed prices).

Then compute ([references/economics.md](references/economics.md)):

```
contribution per unit = price - all variable costs (materials, direct labor, fees, shipping, discounts, refunds)
contribution margin m = contribution / price
max unit loss for a price rise p   = p / (m + p)
units needed for a discount d      = d / (m - d)
profit per visitor or lead = conversion or close rate x contribution per order
required hourly rate = (owner pay + overhead + profit + taxes) / real billable hours
minimum charge >= (round-trip travel + setup + 30 to 60 min work) x loaded rate
subscription lifetime contribution = monthly contribution / monthly churn
break-even annual discount = 1 - ((1 - (1 - c)^12) / c) / 12
add-on value per order = attach rate x add-on contribution
```

Report the three biggest gaps in money, not percentages: underpricing, hidden discounts, missing add-ons, payment leaks.

## Decision rules, in order

1. **Classify each offer before touching price:** underpriced or capacity-constrained; priced right but badly packaged; good core but no add-on revenue; retention or payment leak. No discount until this is done.
2. **Underpriced signals** (close rate above 70 to 80%, waitlist, "very reasonable", no raise in 18 months, resellers marking you up): raise for new customers first, then the installed base with notice. Use the break-even table to size it.
3. **Price from the floor up, never from the competitor down.** Cost floor, value ceiling, market check, then a choice of position ([references/setting-prices.md](references/setting-prices.md)).
4. **Quote with options.** Three real tiers and pre-priced optional lines on every eligible quote. Every price reduction removes scope.
5. **Value-add before discount.** Test in this order: proof, remove a cost or risk, included extra, bundle, cash off last. Same order as the beauty-business and paid-ads skills.
6. **Guarantee the buyer's main fear,** specifically, next to the buy button. Redo guarantees for services, conditional for programs, never income or medical outcomes.
7. **Add one adjacent add-on before payment and one after.** Track attach rate per base product.
8. **Recurring only for natural recurrence and spare capacity.** Intervene before renewal 2; pause and skip next to cancel; card updater and dunning before launch.
9. **Deposits where no-shows or custom work leak money.** Full payment before custom production; prepaid first wholesale order.
10. **Installments for high tickets,** priced 10 to 20% above pay-in-full for your own plans; BNPL judged on net profit after fees.
11. **Review prices on a fixed date every year,** and write increase clauses into new contracts.

## Setting a price in one screen

- **Floor:** contribution per unit with the owner's time at a target wage. Services: required hourly rate and a minimum charge ($130 to $250 per visit for field services, operators report). Products bought with ads: at least $25 and 3x landed cost (operator rule, 282 upvotes); Etsy and print on demand at least $8 to $10 profit after all fees (38 of 50 abandoned POD shops were underpriced, 173 upvotes).
- **Ceiling:** value to the buyer: cost of the alternative, money gained or saved, time freed. Charge a share of it.
- **Market:** mystery-shop 3 to 5 competitors. A cleaning founder called all five local rivals as a customer; none called back, so speed became the positioning.
- **Research:** quote-and-watch or sequential cohorts for services; Van Westendorp for the range; Gabor-Granger "Would you buy this at $X?" on 5 prices with 100+ completes per segment; live A/B judged on contribution per visitor.
- **Structure:** flat job prices from an internal hourly model; price by drivers customers can see (dog count, stories, bedrooms); simple units ($6 per knife beat $1 per inch, 622 upvotes).

## Raising prices in one screen

- New customers first; legacy clients modeled, not grandfathered forever. Departing legacy clients are replaced at current rates.
- Routine: same date every year. Catch-up: 25 to 40% survivable for strong relationships; stage in 10% steps for price-sensitive products.
- Notice 60 to 90 days for recurring clients, 30 days otherwise. Lead with the date and the exact new price, then concrete value, then an easy exit. Offer the old rate for prepayment.
- Pair with visible value when possible: a shaving box expanded its assortment, then went from $12 to $29 a month and conversion rose (1,754 upvotes).
- Judge on revenue retention and contribution over 8 to 12 weeks, not logo churn.

Playbook, outcome table and notice rules: [references/price-increases.md](references/price-increases.md).

## Offer design in one screen

- **Value equation:** (dream outcome x likelihood) / (time x effort). Score each 1 to 10, fix the lowest. Most "too expensive" offers lack proof.
- **Tiers:** Good lean with written exclusions, Better the intended choice, Best with the high-cost conveniences. No decoys built to be ignored.
- **Bonuses:** one per real objection, verifiable value, under 2x the core price, given on completion not on booking.
- **Scarcity:** only real constraints you will enforce: capacity, cohort, season, founding price that actually rises.
- **Bundles:** show parts at full price and the saving as a line; discount only where bundling cuts cost to serve.
- **Upsells:** adjacent problem, low thinking. Second unit post-purchase for a companion took 6% (2,730 upvotes); a cart ladder lifted AOV from $176 to $203 (1,564 upvotes).

## Psychological pricing: what holds

- **Holds:** anchoring with the premium option first, the middle-option compromise effect, "free" extras over equal cash discounts, all-in prices over surprise fees, per-day framing for ongoing plans.
- **Weak or failed:** charm prices (.99) show near-zero effects between subjects in a 2023 meta-analysis and failed a 2022 preregistered replication; decoys are fragile with realistic options; loud "no risk" copy lowered purchases in 2024 field experiments.
- **Regulated:** fake former prices (EU 30-day lowest-price rule, US FTC guides), hidden mandatory fees (UK DMCC from April 2025, California SB 478, US FTC rule for tickets and lodging).

Full evidence table: [references/psychology.md](references/psychology.md).

## When practitioners and the data disagree

| Topic | Practitioners say | Data says | Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intro discounts | Start low to fill, then raise (coaches) | Discount-trained buyers expect more; low marketplace anchors are hard to raise | Off-platform only, shown as a line with a written end date |
| Charm pricing | .99 sells more | Near-zero effect except when the left digit changes | Round for premium and services; test only left-digit moves |
| Free shipping | Include it, sales "skyrocketed" | Fine until heavy or international orders | All-in for single items, threshold for baskets, actual rates for heavy |
| BNPL | +30% conversion vs account loss and early chargebacks | Up to +14% revenue, randomized | Above about $250 only; net the fee premium |
| Size of a raise | Small yearly steps vs one 40% jump | Break-even loss p / (m + p) | Yearly steps by default; catch-up jumps with notice and an exit |
| Guarantees | Big promise wins | Wording wins, badges do not; varies by device | Specific promise near the CTA, test by device |
| Shared-meal or outside-drink fees | Recover margin | Lost a party of 9 (368 upvotes) | Fold into price or skip |

## Never

- Never recommend a price cut without the break-even volume and a profit-per-visitor check.
- Never invent a former price, a "worth $X" bonus value, a deadline or a stock count.
- Never discount for reviews; Google can remove every review and suspend the profile.
- Never hide mandatory fees or bury cancellation; never reset countdown timers.
- Never guarantee income, test scores or medical outcomes.
- Never change a live price, coupon or checkout without the owner's approval, and test the checkout after any change.
- Never send a price-change message to customers without the owner confirming text and recipients.
