# Pricing psychology: what holds, what does not

Treat every "psychological pricing" tactic as a test variable, not a rule. Several famous effects shrink or vanish outside the lab. Each row gives the evidence grade: A = randomized field data or meta-analysis, B = lab research with replications, C = lab or vendor claim with weak or failed replication, P = practitioner report. Sources: [sources.md](sources.md).

## Evidence table

| Tactic | What the evidence says | Grade | Rule for the agent |
|---|---|---|---|
| Charm prices (.99, .95, 9-endings) | 2023 meta-analysis: small, highly heterogeneous effects; between-subject purchase effects near zero; stronger only when the left digit changes ($3.99 vs $4.00). A 2022 preregistered study (266 people, 4,788 decisions) failed to reproduce left-digit or fluency effects | A (both pre-2024) | Optional. Only worth testing when it moves the left digit. Use round prices for premium services, B2B and professional work |
| Decoy (asymmetric dominance) | Robust in classic lab menus but fragile with realistic, perceptual or richly described options (JMR 2014 replication papers). A 2026 commercial "digital twin" decoy test used only 20 forced choices per condition | C | Never build a deliberately useless tier. Build three real options; the middle one wins on its merits |
| Compromise effect (people avoid extremes) | More robust than the decoy in the literature: a middle option gains share when flanked by a cheaper and a pricier one | B | Put the option you want sold in the middle and make it the obvious value. Mark it "most chosen" only if it is |
| Anchoring with a high first number | Robust in lab and negotiation research; precise anchors ($4,850) draw smaller counteroffers than round ones ($5,000) in lab studies | B | Lead quotes and pricing pages with the premium option or the full-scope price. Use precise numbers on quotes you expect to be negotiated |
| Strikethrough "was" prices | Anchors work, but regulated: in the EU a price-reduction claim must show the lowest price of the prior 30 days (Omnibus Directive, applied since 2022); the US FTC guides treat fictitious former prices as deceptive | A (law) | Only show a former price that was genuinely charged for a meaningful period. Never invent a compare-at price |
| Discount depth and frequency | Randomized retailer field test (2023): one 20% coupon raised customers' expected frequency and depth of future promotions; modeled long-run profit loss above 10% when customers get used to frequent discounts | A (pre-2024) | Default to value-adds. Discount with a reason, an end date and a target segment |
| Risk-reassurance copy ("TAKE NO RISK!") | Three retailer field experiments, 452,632+ consumers (2024): aggressive risk-reducing messages lowered engagement and later purchases in some conditions | A | Make guarantees specific and calm. State the mechanism, not the absence of risk |
| Money-back guarantee near the buy button | Agency A/B tests: +30.33% desktop conversion and +20.03% revenue, but worse on mobile (Blend Commerce); guarantee copy alone +28.34% add-to-cart, trust badges alone -6.61% not significant (Ingversions) | C (single tests, agency bias) | The promise sells, the badge does not. Test by device |
| Free (zero-price effect) | Lab studies show "free" is valued beyond its price difference | B | "Free installation" or "free second item" usually beats an equal cash discount. Check the cost |
| Partitioned price (item + shipping) vs all-in | Operators report abandonment when shipping is shown separately: $10 + $4 shipping vs $14 free shipping, sales "skyrocketed" after switching (P). A $30 + $20 shipping product moved to about $60 with free shipping and sales tripled (P) | P | All-in prices with free shipping for single-item buys; a threshold for multi-item baskets |
| Drip pricing (mandatory fees revealed late) | Raises short-term purchases in research but now banned in many places: UK DMCC Act (from April 2025), US FTC fees rule for live-event tickets and short-term lodging (May 2025), California SB 478 (July 2024) | A (law) | Show every mandatory fee in the first price the customer sees |
| Per-day or per-use framing ("$1.30 a day") | Lab evidence that small-unit framing lowers perceived cost for ongoing spending | B | Use for memberships and subscriptions; always also show the real billed amount and interval |
| Price as a quality signal | Van Westendorp's "too cheap to trust" question exists because very low prices raise doubt; operators report homeowners stopped haggling after a plumber wrapped his trucks (P) | B/P | Underpricing can lose sales. Test higher before lower |
| Rule of 100 (percent off under $100, dollars off over $100) | Popular heuristic, no strong test found | C | Use the bigger-looking number; do not treat as proven |
| Odd small bags vs one big shared unit | Six $4 bags beat one $25 tub at family events: each child gets one, replacements get bought (P, 9 upvotes) | P | For shared consumption, sell individual units |

## Framing that is cheap and usually safe

- **Price the difference, not the plan.** "+$40 for same-day service", "+$12 a month for unlimited users", "save $240 a year on annual". Buyers evaluate the step, not the column of features (research report, 2026).
- **Show the full price, then the discount line, then the reason.** It keeps the reference price intact for the next sale (r/smallbusiness, 50 upvotes, two independent agreements).
- **Present the bundle as main item plus free extra.** "Get A for $45 and we will include B and free shipping" instead of "A and B for $45 + $4.99 shipping" (r/dropship, 387 upvotes; a commenter noted it gives up $4.99 per order, so test it on profit).
- **"Free trailer" reframing.** A boat that sat unsold for months sold within a week after the price went up and the trailer was called free (secondhand story, 88 upvotes). Plausible mechanism, weak evidence.
- **Monthly equivalent and total.** On annual plans show both "$25/month" and "$300 billed yearly" so the discount is real and the charge is not a surprise.

## What to stop doing

- Repricing everything to .99 and calling it optimization.
- A "Pro" tier no one should buy, created only to make another tier look good.
- Permanent "sale" banners. One frequent Etsy buyer refuses to shop stores that always have a sale because the pricing looks dishonest (16 upvotes).
- Countdown timers that reset, "only 2 left" when there are 200, fake former prices. Besides the legal risk, they teach customers to wait or distrust.
- Badges as a substitute for a real guarantee.

## How to test a psychological tweak

1. One variable at a time, same traffic source and headline.
2. Judge on profit per visitor and refund rate, not click-through or add-to-cart.
3. Run until each arm has at least 100 conversions, or treat the result as a hint.
4. Split by device: the same guarantee placement won on desktop and lost on mobile.
5. Log hypothesis, dates, variants, conversions, revenue and profit per visitor, refunds, decision.
