# Setting a price: cost floor, value ceiling, market check, then research

A price sits between a floor (what it costs you to deliver profitably) and a ceiling (what the outcome is worth to the buyer). The market tells you where buyers anchor, not where you must land. Formulas: [economics.md](economics.md). Sources: [sources.md](sources.md).

## Step 1. The floor: costs

- Compute contribution per unit with every variable cost, including the owner's own hours at a target wage ([economics.md](economics.md) section 1).
- Services: required hourly rate from annual costs and real billable hours; minimum charge from travel and setup; long work priced by the hour so a six-hour job earns at least six one-hour jobs (hair correction rates of $100 to $125 an hour are common; juniors charging $130 for eight hours is the classic mistake, r/hairstylist).
- Products: landed cost, then fees, shipping, returns and ad cost per order.
- Price floors operators use when every order is bought with ads:

| Rule | Context | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Price at least $25 and 3x cost | DTC with Facebook ads; $10 to $20 accessories at 2 to 3x struggled | 282 upvotes |
| $20+ profit per order before ads; breakeven ROAS = price / profit before ads ($29.99 / $20 = 1.5) | Dropshipping | 141 upvotes, flagged as promotional |
| Amazon gate: price x 0.85 - FBA fee - 2 months storage - COGS leaves above 45% gross margin | Amazon private label | 124 upvotes |
| Retail at 3 to 4x factory cost | $10M Amazon seller | 5 upvotes |
| At least $8 to $10 profit after production, shipping and fees | Etsy print on demand. 38 of 50 abandoned POD shops were underpriced (median $17.99 on $12 to $14 cost); shops with 500 to 5,000 sales had median prices of $26 to $32 | 173 upvotes, correlation only |
| Work backward: (production + shipping + target profit) / (1 - fee share). ($12 + $4.50 + $8) / 0.75 = $32.67, list $32.99 | Etsy POD | 22 upvotes |

Floors relax when demand arrives without ad spend: a $10 Amazon item at 35% margin made about $3.50 a sale at 50 units a day (237 upvotes). Thin margins also work at high ticket and fast turnover: a watch wholesaler targets 6 to 12% on $10,000 average items and netted $63,000 on $1.2M sales (247 upvotes).

## Step 2. The ceiling: value to the buyer

Ask what the buyer gains or avoids, in their own money or time:

- **Cost of the alternative.** "$7,000 coaching vs $30,000 divorce court" (r/lifecoaching, 53 upvotes, illustrative). The cost of a botched cheap contractor, a missed season, a failed launch.
- **Economic value:** extra revenue, saved hours x their hourly value, avoided fines or repairs. A copywriter qualifies course sellers by revenue capacity: at $1,000 to $2,000 per course and 100 visitors a day, 5% conversion is $5,000 a day (73 upvotes, strongly disputed thread).
- **Outcome over input.** Coaches who sold one $2,000 to $3,000 outcome package ("lower turnover by how much") instead of hourly sessions report easier sales (17 upvotes). A $2,500 quarterly contract with weekly meetings works out to about $208 an hour (187 upvotes).
- **Price to the buyer's stakes, not your effort.** A copywriter who charged $250 to $400 per sales page quoted "$7,500" to a new inquiry; it was accepted within minutes with fewer revisions (432 upvotes, one anecdote).

The ceiling for a segment is roughly the value of the outcome minus what the buyer must also spend or risk to get it. Charge a share of the value, not all of it.

## Step 3. The market check

- **Mystery-shop competitors.** Call or request quotes as a prospective customer: rates, availability, response time. A cleaning founder called all five local competitors; none called back. Responsiveness became the positioning and pricing room (r/sweatystartup, 13 upvotes). Pressure washers have "casual pricing conversations" with competitors before launching and got chemical sourcing tips too.
- **Benchmark from where you worked.** A pressure washer priced gas stations at $500 each from what his former employer charged and landed three (r/pressurewashing).
- **Category scan for products.** Record the top 100 sellers' prices and last-month units in the category before a change; in one 2025 tariff scan only 3 of 100 had raised prices (152 upvotes).
- **Read advertised luxury prices carefully.** A $10,000 "starting at" wedding price can cover three days, and photographers advertising $10,000+ accepted $5,000 budgets privately (124 upvotes).
- **Marketplace ranges are floors to escape.** Tutors on Italki in one market charge $15 to $30 an hour; start in range, move off-platform, raise as demand builds (83 upvotes). Low marketplace anchors are hard to raise later: a Preply tutor on a $12 suggested rate netted about $8 (72 upvotes).
- **Do not match a discounting competitor by reflex.** Check the break-even table first. Several Amazon sellers raised instead of matching: one ASIN went from $39.99 to $59.99 and did $200,000 in 5 months against $150,000 the prior year (11 upvotes, positioning changed too).

## Step 4. Research willingness to pay (cheap methods first)

### Ranked by cost

1. **Quote-and-watch for services.** Quote new prospects at the new price for 4 to 8 weeks, track close rate, average job value and lead quality. Change one thing at a time. A mobile car wash commenter proposed $100 for a first wash and $80 for repeats on new customers only, raising again if demand holds (19 upvotes).
2. **Sequential cohorts.** Old price for month 1, new price for month 2, same channels. For services, this avoids showing two prices to neighbors at once.
3. **Raise until demand bends.** Handmade knifemaker raised step by step as people kept buying, up to several hundred dollars per knife (995 upvotes). An Etsy seller went $16.99 to $19.99 after the first 20 sales, tested $24.99, saw sales drop and returned to $19.99 (207 upvotes). A test where almost everyone says yes teaches nothing.
4. **Customer and lost-prospect interviews.** Ask after showing the full offer: what they compared you with, what they expected to pay, what nearly stopped them.
5. **Van Westendorp survey** for the acceptable range. Four questions, after the offer is shown in full:
   - "At what price would this be so inexpensive you would question its quality?"
   - "At what price would this be a bargain?"
   - "At what price would it start to feel expensive, but still be worth considering?"
   - "At what price would you no longer consider buying?"
   Plot the curves; the crossing points give a range. It says what feels acceptable, not what maximizes profit (SurveyMonkey, 2026).
6. **Gabor-Granger** for the revenue-maximizing price of one fixed offer: "Would you buy this at $X?" on a 5-price ladder, with the offer held constant. At least 100 completes per segment (SurveyMonkey, Aug 2026). 100 responses near 50% acceptance gives about +/-9.8 points of error; 400 gives +/-4.9 (Sprig, Oct 2026). Multiply each price by acceptance to get the revenue curve; multiply by contribution, not price, to get the profit curve.
7. **Live A/B price test** (ecommerce and self-serve software): randomize qualified traffic to materially different prices, judge on contribution per visitor, run to at least 100 orders per arm. Hold the traffic source and headline constant.
8. **Pre-sell.** Take deposits or pre-orders at the target price before producing. Easter baskets sold by preorder from a preset menu before buying supplies (suggestion); a candle maker's 25-seat classes at an undisclosed price filled almost every time (1,888 upvotes).

### Research traps

- Asking price before describing the offer measures nothing.
- Surveys overstate willingness; treat Gabor-Granger acceptance as an upper bound and discount it.
- Interest is not demand. 30+ comments on a mini-session interest post turned into "little response once dates and prices were posted" (77 upvotes). Only paid bookings count.
- Publishing prices filters. One coach saw enquiries fall 75% after publishing base prices while good leads stayed flat (3 upvotes, others agreed). Fewer, better enquiries is usually the win for a capacity-limited business.

## Step 5. Choose and structure

- **Pick the price from the floor up, never from the competitor down.** Then decide position: below market only with a structural cost edge; at market with a clear difference; above market with proof, speed, guarantee or specialization.
- **Quote by the job, not the hour,** for services the customer cannot judge in hours. Flat monthly pricing on a one-year auto-renewing contract is how one commercial cleaner prices 98% of accounts, from a spreadsheet of service-cost inputs plus markup (1,990 upvotes).
- **Simplify units.** A knife sharpener moved from $1 per inch to $6 per knife and $6 to $8 per tool; typical orders became 4 to 10 items, $24 to $60 (622 upvotes).
- **Price by the cost driver customers can see.** Pet waste by dog count, not yard size (24 upvotes). House washing by stories and access, not only square footage (14 upvotes plus agreeing replies).
- **Same price for shorter sessions** when admin and scheduling are fixed: a 30-minute coaching session priced like 50 to 60 minutes (10 upvotes).
- **Quote high enough to leave room only when you expect to negotiate.** Pressure washer quotes $350 for a driveway he would take at $250 (3 upvotes). Better: three predefined margin levels and scope cuts for any reduction ([economics.md](economics.md) section 12).
- **Price difficult prospects up, or decline.** Multiply rates 2 to 5x for prospects who show difficult behavior early, all terms in writing (4 upvotes); several photographers report discounted clients complain most.

## Re-check after launch

Track for 8 to 12 weeks: close or conversion rate, average order or job value, contribution per lead or visitor, mix of customers (are the good ones staying), refund and complaint rate, capacity used. Log every price change with date, old and new price, and reason.
