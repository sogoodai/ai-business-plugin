# Price increase playbook

Most small businesses raise too late and too little, then make one painful catch-up jump. 65% of US home-service businesses raised prices in the prior year, 80% of those above $500,000 revenue (Jobber, Dec 2025). The question is rarely "can we raise?" but "who, by how much, when, and how do we say it?" Scripts in full: [scripts.md](scripts.md). Sources: [sources.md](sources.md).

## Signals that the price is too low

- Close rate on quotes above about 70 to 80%, or nearly everyone says yes without comparing.
- Fully booked with a waitlist, or weeks of lead time.
- Customers say "that's very reasonable" or tip heavily.
- Costs rose and margin fell below the plan.
- No increase in 18+ months.
- Resellers buy from you to resell at a markup. One maker's goods were being resold on Amazon at 3x; after a price rise the reseller disappeared (r/Etsy, 7 upvotes).
- The hardest, most labor-intensive item sells the most. Etsy sellers who doubled the price of their most cumbersome bestseller reported sales doubling or tripling (15 and 252 upvotes; listing refresh may explain part of it).

## Decide who and how much

1. **Compute the break-even loss** for the proposed rise at the current margin ([economics.md](economics.md) section 2). At 50% margin, +20% survives losing 29% of units.
2. **New customers first.** Raise the price for new customers and quotes now; decide on the installed base separately. Publishers that raised new-subscriber prices showed 18.7% median revenue growth versus 12% for existing-subscriber increases (Piano, 2026, observational).
3. **By capacity and demand, not across the board.** The full provider, the waitlisted service, the peak slot goes up first. A painting contractor holds his top margin when the schedule is loaded and only drops a level to fill an unsold slot (222 upvotes).
4. **Legacy clients:** model them explicitly. A lawn operator kept his second customer at $55 a week for four years against $120 market quotes because he "felt bad" (12 upvotes). Departing legacy-price clients get replaced at current rates: "someone waiting whenever a client left who would pay substantially more" (r/hairstylist, 52 upvotes).
5. **Size of step:**
   - Routine: a fixed review date every year. Operators: same percentage on the same date every year avoids catch-up shocks (94 upvotes); massage therapists report 5 to 8% every 18 to 20 months with notice (operator report). SaaS: 29% raise annually, 21% at renewal (SBI, 2025).
   - Catch-up after years flat: 25 to 40% is survivable for services with a strong relationship, see measured outcomes below. Stage it if the base is price-sensitive: one Amazon brand raised 50 to 60% over about 8 months in 10% steps, checking conversion between steps (136 upvotes).
   - Cost shock (tariffs, materials): pass through the dollar increase, or take part of it. One seller raised about 13% while competitors went up 25 to 55% (79 upvotes). A coffee shop added a temporary 1% surcharge on everything after coffee costs rose (19 upvotes).
6. **Pair the raise with visible value when you can.** A shaving subscription expanded the box before going from $12 to $29 (1,754 upvotes); a brand raised 12% in January alongside new colors and a redesign (56 upvotes).
7. **Repackage instead of a flat raise** when customers differ: a lighter tier at the old price, the full service at the new one. A telecom SaaS repackaged and migrated prices: ARPU EUR 15 to 21, MRR +50%, churn 2% to 3.3% (Valueships, 2024, agency case). Churn up and revenue up together is a win; judge on revenue retention, not logo count.

Operators who raise almost never report losing more than the break-even share. The usual regret is waiting too long ("waiting 6 years for the first increase was a mistake").

## Notice and timing

- **Notice:** 30 days for product and menu prices (or none for retail shelf prices); 60 to 90 days for retainers, memberships and recurring services; at contract renewal for annual contracts.
- **Contracts:** write an annual increase clause at signing ("fees increase by up to 10% each January"). An agency practitioner says fee increases about every 3 years "usually more than offset losing one or two clients" (7 upvotes); another regretted not adding a 10% yearly clause earlier (11 and 18 upvotes).
- **Seasonality:** raise going into peak demand, not into a slump. Stylists argue February (before tax refunds) versus April (41 upvotes); neither has data. Pick the start of your busiest quarter.
- **Long-lead bookings:** add a price valid-until date or a materials clause to quotes. A florist at 10% margin lost money on weddings contracted before wholesale prices rose (175 upvotes).
- **Date every downloadable menu and price list.** Old third-party copies cause disputes (a customer expected a $10 burger from 2014).

## How to say it

Lead with the date and the exact new price, then concrete value or cost reasons, then an easy path. Never apologize at length or invite negotiation.

- **Recurring service email (measured above):** "Hey [name], I wanted to give you a heads up that starting January my rates will be [new rate]. This reflects the growth in my skills, the results I've been delivering for your business, and an adjustment to market rates. I completely understand if this doesn't fit your budget and I'm happy to recommend someone else if needed. If you'd like to continue working together at the new rate, no action needed on your end." (r/smallbusiness, 196 upvotes; one client praised its professionalism.)
- **Subscription or membership:** "On [date], your price will change from [old] to [new]. Since your current price was set, we've added [specific benefits], improved [measurable service level], and absorbed higher [cost, if true]. Your service and billing terms otherwise stay the same. If you'd like to review the best plan for your current usage before the change, reply to this email." (research report, 2026)
- **Prepay to lock:** offer the old price for clients who prepay a block or leave a non-refundable deposit before the effective date (suggestion, 11 upvotes). It converts resistance into cash.
- **Show the math when challenged:** stylists who keep a yearly cost spreadsheet and show it report the conversation "takes emotion out" (4 upvotes).
- **In person, short:** "Yes, my prices went up this year like everything else. Same care, same time with you." Then move on.

More variants (one-line text, price-shopper, tariff line, ending a friend rate): [scripts.md](scripts.md).

## What not to do

- A surprise increase at checkout. Always notice in writing first.
- Defending a big increase in public review replies with expansion costs or Michelin comparisons; it made things worse (r/restaurantowners, 13 upvotes). Reply briefly, offer to talk privately.
- Grandfathering forever by default. Calculate the lifetime cost of the legacy rate first.
- Raising every provider or product by the same percentage when demand differs.
- Raising on Amazon without checking Buy Box eligibility and your prices on other channels: Amazon suppresses offers priced above what it finds elsewhere, including per-unit comparisons across pack sizes (operator reports).
- Cutting price back at the first complaint. A Toronto nail tech asked peers before lowering after one complaint; the market validated her 90 to 180 CAD sets and she kept them (7,676 upvotes).

## After the change

Watch 8 to 12 weeks: churn or cancellations by segment, revenue retention, close rate, new-client mix, complaints. If churn exceeds the break-even share, offer the leavers a lighter tier, not the old price.
