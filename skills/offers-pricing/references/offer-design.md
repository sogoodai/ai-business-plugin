# Offer design: value, tiers, guarantees, bonuses, scarcity, discounts, bundles, upsells

An offer is what the buyer gets, how risky it feels and how they pay. Price is only compared against it. Frameworks below adapt MIT-licensed work from coreyhaines31/marketingskills, coleschaffer/dtc-copywriting-skills, alirezarezvani/claude-skills and mohitagw15856/pm-claude-skills (see [sources.md](sources.md)); numbers carry their source.

## 1. Value equation: diagnose before discounting

Value = (dream outcome x perceived likelihood of getting it) / (time to result x effort and sacrifice). Credited to Alex Hormozi, *$100M Offers*; scoring method adapted from coreyhaines31/marketingskills (MIT).

Score each lever 1 to 10 from the buyer's point of view. Fix the lowest one first, one lever per iteration.

| Lever | Ask | Typical fixes |
|---|---|---|
| Dream outcome | Can the buyer picture the day after it works and repeat it to a friend? | Name the outcome concretely: "driveway like new by Saturday", "first 10 students in 60 days" |
| Likelihood | Are there 3+ comparable proof points, a named method, an answer to "my case is different"? | Before and afters from similar customers, named process, specific guarantee |
| Time to result | When is the first concrete win? | Engineer a win in week 1, same-day or next-day service, start date on the quote |
| Effort | What work, decisions and risk does the buyer carry? | Done-for-you, fewer choices, install included, pickup and drop-off |

Most "we need to lower the price" problems are a weak likelihood lever: the buyer does not believe it will work for them. "Most stuck offers need proof, not features" (coreyhaines31). Expect 10 to 40% relative lift per component change, multiplicative, not additive (coreyhaines31 heuristic, uncited); anyone promising 5x is selling something.

### Offer anatomy checklist

1. Core deliverable stated as an outcome, with "includes" and "does not include".
2. Bonuses that each kill one objection (section 5).
3. Guarantee matched to the buyer's main fear (section 4).
4. Real scarcity or urgency, or none (section 6).
5. A name a customer could repeat: "I signed up for the [name], it's $X and I get [outcome]".
6. Price and payment structure (section 3 and [recurring-and-payments.md](recurring-and-payments.md)).

## 2. Value-adds before discounts

A cash discount lowers the reference price for next time; a value-add does not. In a randomized retailer test, one 20% coupon raised customers' expectations of future discount frequency and depth, with modeled long-run profit loss above 10% when customers become trained (2023).

Order to test offers in (consistent with the beauty-business and paid-ads skills):

1. **Proof and specificity, no discount.**
2. **Remove a cost or risk:** creditable consult, deposit credited to the job, free trip charge with a booked job, guarantee.
3. **Included extra:** add-on service, free gift, free install, extra unit, priority slot. A clothing brand found "free gift with your first order" beat discounts, bundles and BOGO (r/FacebookAds, 163 upvotes, no figures). Estheticians: give a free scrub, sunscreen or samples instead of a % off the service (77 upvotes).
4. **Bundle or quantity offer:** buy 2 get 1, kit price.
5. **Cash discount** last, with a reason, an end date and a target segment.

Legitimate reasons for a cash discount: clearing aged inventory, filling known dead capacity (weekday, off-season), annual prepay, a measured acquisition offer with proven repeat economics, a tightly targeted win-back, a once-a-year event.

### Promotion cadence

- **One or two real sale events a year beat always-on discounts.** A store running 25% off everything once a year on Black Friday did $12,099 in a day at 15.34% conversion against a normal 3 to 5% (r/ecommerce, 193 upvotes). Dropping an always-on 10% discount showed no large sales change (82 upvotes).
- **Shallow beats deep:** about 15% off grew BFCM gross profit 2.05x versus 1.75x at about 40% off (Lebesgue 2025, cited in the ecommerce-brand skill).
- **Escalate by stage, not by calendar:** full price to new visitors, small incentive on retargeting, deepest offer only to people who saw two ads and did not buy (117 upvotes).
- **Flat-dollar beat percent** on Amazon coupons for struggling products, nearly doubling conversion (24 upvotes). Rule of 100 (percent under $100, dollars over) is a heuristic.
- **Off-peak specials** for capacity only: "2 for 1 dinner, $25", "$6 cocktails 3 to 6 pm" (restaurant operators), weekday elopements so weekends stay open for full weddings (photographers).
- **Fake coupon at full price** (dotted border, no discount): 23 redemptions in 35 minutes, no complaints (38-year restaurant owner, 9 upvotes). The format, not the discount, drove action.
- **Discount pop-ups:** several operators replaced them with early access or content and saw pop-up conversion fall but total revenue rise (7 upvotes). If kept, show on exit intent after 2+ product views.

## 3. Tiers and anchors

### When to tier

- Every quote where the customer could reasonably choose more or less scope. Only 16% of home-service operators use good-better-best while optional quote items are accepted 25 to 50% of the time (Jobber 2026).
- Thriving contractors present three options on half of jobs versus under 10% of struggling ones (ServiceTitan 2025, correlation, cited in home-services).

### How to build three tiers

- **Good** solves the core problem, deliberately lean, with exclusions written out.
- **Better** adds the most-requested upgrade. This is where most buyers should land; mark it "most chosen" only if true.
- **Best** adds the high-cost, high-convenience items (warranty, priority, done-for-you, premium materials) and makes Better look efficient while still being worth buying for high-value customers.
- Price ratios used as priors: Good to Better about 2 to 2.5x and Better to Best about 2x for software (alirezarezvani, MIT; aaron-he-zhu, Apache-2.0, labels its 1x / 2.2x / 5x as community heuristics). For service quotes the steps are usually smaller (repair / repair + prevention / replacement).
- Every upgrade needs one trigger a buyer can name ("you have more than 3 staff", "you want it done this week").
- **Anti-patterns** (alirezarezvani, MIT): Best priced over 2x Better with under 1.5x the value; a feature in all three tiers; a Good tier that costs over 80% of its price to serve; "call us" with no "starting at" price.
- Never build a decoy no one should buy. Decoy effects are fragile in realistic choices ([psychology.md](psychology.md)).

### Anchors

- Show the premium option or full scope first; the next option then reads as sensible.
- Anchor to the cost of the alternative or of inaction, not to an invented "worth $X".
- Price the step, not the column: "+$40 for same-day".
- Real reference prices only; EU law requires the lowest price of the prior 30 days for any discount claim.

### Service-specific tier patterns that worked

- **Premium package from repeated requests.** A mobile beer bar hired a certified bartender when customers asked for cocktails and sold it as a higher package; "business started booming", booked almost every weekend (1,637 upvotes).
- **Predefined packages instead of custom quotes.** Trophy shop: four tournament packages from a club trophy plus top-three trophies and medals down to medals only (9 upvotes, proposal).
- **Fewer hours, not a lower rate.** Photographers offer 5 hours instead of 10 for tight budgets (13 upvotes); prospects are often surprised it is an option.
- **Restricted basic tier with paid change requests** instead of lowering the existing service (5 upvotes).
- **Remove the smallest package** if it lets peak dates go to low-value bookings (wedding photographer, 48 upvotes). Or set a minimum: one photographer moved all packages to 8 hours after a 4-hour booking ran late.
- **Longer sessions at a lower hourly rate:** massage 60 min $100, 90 min $130, 120 min $175 (102 upvotes).

## 4. Guarantees and risk reversal

A guarantee works when it removes the specific fear blocking the purchase. The promise sells; the badge does not ([psychology.md](psychology.md)).

### Types (taxonomy adapted from coreyhaines31/marketingskills, MIT)

| Type | Works for | Backfires when |
|---|---|---|
| Unconditional money-back ("30 days, no questions") | Low-price products, info products, high trust | High-touch services, consumables used up |
| Conditional ("complete the steps, then refund if no result") | Courses, programs needing effort | Sophisticated buyers who read it as a trap |
| Redo or make-it-right | Cleaning, detailing, repairs, beauty | Vague standard of "right" |
| Better than money back (refund + extra) | Confident delivery, ample margin | Failures go public |
| Service-level ("not done by Friday, X% off") | Productized services, agencies, trades | Vague deadlines |
| Performance or pay-on-result | B2B with measurable, attributable outcomes | Long or hard-to-attribute cycles |
| Outcome or keep working free | Coaching, marketing retainers | Open-ended cost |
| Long labor warranty | HVAC, roofing, installs: one contractor offered 10 years of labor versus a 1 to 2 year norm and says it ended price competition (481 upvotes, authenticity disputed) | Workmanship you cannot control |
| No refunds, stated plainly | Premium, mature buyers, events with fixed costs | First-time buyers in the category |

### Decision tree

1. **Biggest buyer fear?** "Won't work" leads to money-back or conditional. "Won't be done on time" leads to service-level. "I'll pay and get nothing" leads to performance or keep-working. "Is this legit?" leads to specificity and comparison proof.
2. **Refund tolerance?** Compute the break-even claim rate ([economics.md](economics.md) section 10). Stress-test: what if 10% claim?
3. **Buyer sophistication?** First-time buyers need a strong refund; B2B prefers SLAs; premium buyers can take a clear no-refund policy with strong proof.
4. **Outcome measurable?** Clean metric allows performance terms; fuzzy outcomes stay in the money-back family with a condition.

### Rules

- At most two conditions; write the claim window and the remedy.
- Guarantee what you control: work, timing, redo. Never guarantee income, test scores or medical outcomes (education-coaching skill agrees).
- For services a redo guarantee is usually safer than a refund and matches the failure mode.
- Place it next to the buy button or on the quote, not in the footer. Calm wording; "TAKE NO RISK!" style copy reduced purchases in field experiments (2024).
- Honor what you advertise. A lash tech who advertised free 48-hour touch-ups and then offered half off a fill instead triggered a planned chargeback and review (258 upvotes).
- Amazon sellers: a competitor reported "money back guarantee" in product images and two top listings were removed for days (139 upvotes). Check marketplace image rules.
- "Strong proof plus a modest guarantee beats a strong guarantee with weak proof" (coreyhaines31).
- Re-read guarantee wording every 6 months.

## 5. Bonuses

Bonuses answer objections; they are not padding (adapted from coreyhaines31 and coleschaffer, MIT).

1. Find the objections: last 6 months of lost deals, refund requests and sales calls, plus "What almost made you not buy?" to 3 recent buyers. They cluster into 3 to 6.
2. One bonus per objection: no time leads to done-for-you setup; fear of getting stuck leads to support access; "tried before" leads to a case study like theirs; "won't finish" leads to a check-in.
3. Prefer bonuses with high perceived value and low delivery cost: templates, checklists, recorded training, community access, samples from suppliers. A salon proposed gift packs of supplier samples at a stated "$19.99 value", while supplies last (117 upvotes).
4. Value claims must be verifiable: you sell it separately or a comparable product sells at that price. The two public frameworks disagree on the ceiling (under 2x the core price vs a 10:1 stack); for small businesses keep stated bonus value under 2x the core price and never invent "worth $X".
5. Count: 2 to 5. If the offer would sell on the bonuses alone, fix the core.
6. Trial gifts are given on completion, not booking: a free guitar for booking a trial produced 2 bookings, 0 attended, both kept the guitar (23 upvotes).

## 6. Honest scarcity and urgency

Use only constraints that are real, stated, verifiable and enforced (adapted from coreyhaines31, MIT).

| Format | Example wording |
|---|---|
| Capacity | "I take 4 new clients a month. Next opening: [date]." |
| Cohort or season | "Spring aeration books until April 15. After that, next slot is fall." |
| Founding price | "$49/month for the first 50 members, then $79." Then actually raise it |
| Inventory | "40 made. No restock this season." |
| Bonus deadline | "Book by Friday and the [bonus] is included." |
| Price increase with lock | "Prices go up on [date]. Book before and keep this rate for 12 months." |

- Kill resetting countdown timers, a static "only 3 left", fake viewer counts and repeated "last chance".
- Skip scarcity for cancel-anytime subscriptions, cheap impulse buys and luxury positioning.
- With nothing scarce, use the cost of waiting: "each month the gutters overflow costs [x]", "start today, results by [date]".
- Scarcity closes; it does not create value. Fix the value equation first.

## 7. Bundles

- **Bundle to raise order value or lower decision effort,** not to dump stock. Kit pricing at 5 to 10% off the sum is the usual range (finsilabs, MIT; ecommerce-brand skill).
- **Show the parts at full price and the bundle saving as a line** ("Value $149, bundle $119, save $30"). A pressure washer quoted house and driveway at $900 separately and $840 together; when the prospect pushed for $800 he walked away because he did not need the job (191 upvotes).
- **"Main item plus a free extra"** framing often reads better than a combined price (387 upvotes; costs the shipping line in that example, so test on profit).
- **Annual service bundle** paid monthly for seasonal trades (landscaper, 63 upvotes).
- **Bundle the take-home product into the service price.** A stylist's proposal added $50 to $350 to $500 chemical services and included shampoo and conditioner; no objections, and clients kept buying the line (6 and 9 upvotes).
- **Fold small extras into the item price** rather than charging separately: restaurants that raised mussels $3 and included the bread saw no sales drop (9 and 19 upvotes); a $2 dressing charge lost a regular.
- **Discount bundles only when bundling lowers the cost to serve:** route density, one trip, one shipment, one setup. This reconciles the sibling skills (education-coaching: no bulk discount; home-services: lower per-visit price for higher frequency).
- **Individual units for shared consumption:** six $4 popcorn bags beat one $25 tub at family events (9 upvotes).

## 8. Upsells, add-ons and order bumps

Benchmarks: optional quote items 25 to 50% accepted (Jobber 2026); post-purchase take rate median 21.6%, top quartile 30%, session value +18% median (Funnelish 2026). Older ReConvert data: 4.7% average one-click post-purchase conversion (2023), so denominators differ.

### Placement rule

- **Before payment** when the add-on helps choose the right solution (warranty, installation, the next tier).
- **After payment** when it is genuinely optional and would distract from the close (second unit, accessory, refill).
- **In the quote** as optional, pre-priced lines.
- **During the service** only for what the customer can see: a pressure washer accidentally cleaned part of a pool deck, showed the contrast, and sold the rest (344 upvotes). Drain cleaners sell camera inspections by explaining why the clog keeps returning (5 upvotes).

### What to offer

- **Adjacent problem, low thinking:** installation with equipment, debris haul-away with gutter cleaning ($20 to $50, operator), yard deodorizer with pet waste service, tables and chairs with bounce houses (192 and 50 upvotes), uplighting with DJ service, an extended gallery with wedding photos.
- **Order bump at 20 to 40% of the front-end price** (coleschaffer, MIT). Cross-sell under 50% of the main item.
- **Second identical unit at a deep discount post-purchase,** pitched for a companion: 6% take rate (2,730 upvotes).
- **Discounted post-purchase version for buyers who declined the pre-purchase upsell** (same store).
- **Cart ladder:** free shipping, then free shipping + 10%, then + 13% as items are added, matched to cart contents; AOV $176 to $203 and $8,873 extra sales (1,564 upvotes).
- **Thank-you page one-time offer** of both products at 40 to 60% off: 2 in 10 accepted where 1 in 10 was the plan; when acquisition cost rose from $11 to $14, the front offer alone would have lost $2.50 per sale (207 upvotes). Upsells are what let you afford ads.
- **Paid file or extras gallery:** watermarked low-res rejected wedding images at up to $50 each (only 1 request in 500+ weddings, 54 upvotes); edited extras at $2.50 each or a discounted album; a $1,200 "all files" release fee that no client has taken. Prices on extras protect scope more than they earn.
- **Paid catch-up services:** charge separately for removing old gutter guards, deep-clean first visits, first spring cleanup after a winter pause.

### Rules

- Track attach rate per base product; promote a high-attach add-on to a default option on that product.
- Never pick the upsell by margin alone if it is unrelated.
- Always a visible "no thanks". Limit checkout add-ons to 1 or 2.
- Re-test checkout after any price change: a rotated price ID broke every checkout silently for 14 days on one store (42 upvotes).
