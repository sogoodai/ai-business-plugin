# Farmers markets, events, sampling and cottage food

Markets and events are the cheapest place to measure what ecommerce analytics cannot: taste rejection, whether the pack is understood in three seconds, willingness to pay, flavor ranking and the exact words customers use. Treat each market day as revenue plus research, not as proof that the brand is ready for paid acquisition.

## What a market day is worth

- **Measured:** 9,371 vendor-day observations from 399 vendors at five Oregon markets: beginning vendors averaged $385 per market day, established vendors $489, farm-product vendors $521; attendance raised sales, rain cut them (2024 study of 2019 to 2021 data, COVID-distorted, possibly outdated).
- **Operators report:** a hot sauce maker sold 83 bottles and 5 T-shirts at the Des Moines farmers market on a slow day when a food festival ran a few blocks away (r/hotsauce, 1,405-pt thread). A candle maker estimated 98% of sales came from markets, about $25,000 and 675 units in five months; another sold nearly half of 207 first-year units at four events (r/candlemaking, 614-pt thread).
- **Business cards convert poorly:** one market vendor handed out about 30 cards each Saturday and got 1 to 2 bookings, 3 to 7% (operators report, r/sweatystartup). Capture a phone or email at the table instead.

## Fees and the go or no-go test

| Market fee model | Example | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Flat day stall | $40 a day for a 10x10 (Salt Lake City, 2025) | market schedule |
| Percent of sales, value-added | 10% of daily sales (Salt Lake City, 2025) | market schedule |
| Percent by product type | 8% bakery and confectionery, 10% hot food, or a minimum artisan rate (Portland, 2025 to 2026) | market schedule |
| State averages | about $25 a day, 5% where percentage-based (Vermont, 2019, small sample) | NOFA-VT survey |

Rule: if booth fee plus commission would exceed 15% of the sales you expect, test a smaller or cheaper market first (2026 vendor guide, low-grade source). Then run the break-even in [economics.md](economics.md#waterfall-7-market-day).

## Before you book

- **Check the competing calendar.** Festivals, games and other food events nearby pull traffic.
- **Check the booth position.** One vendor's stall sat behind two others, opposite a pillar, next to an aggressive perfume sampler that made shoppers reroute; it was barely visible (operators report, r/jewelrymaking 486-pt thread). Ask for the map and walk the lane.
- **Check sampling rules.** Many markets and health departments require a separate sampling permit, handwashing setup, covered samples and single-use utensils. Ask the market manager and the county.
- **Check what the license covers.** Cottage food rules may forbid certain products at markets or forbid shipping after the market (see below).

## Booth tactics that operators report

- **Six to eight SKUs, not twenty.** A candle maker launched with 5 to 6 scents in two sizes and called it manageable; a perfumer's advice to another maker was 10 SKUs total including seasonals, with most sales in a few (operators report). Fewer facings read as premium.
- **Leave empty space.** A crowded table looks cheap; copy the spacing of the best local boutique (operators report, r/candlemaking).
- **Make sampling clean.** Glass cloches over scent testers keep them concentrated and dust-free; a paper wrap around display soaps lets people smell without residue on their hands (operators report). For food: covered portions, toothpicks or spoons, a waste bin, and the price on the sample sign.
- **Vote, don't ask.** A candle maker brought unreleased holiday scents to an October market and asked every visitor to smell and vote; the winner became a top holiday seller (operators report). Do the same with flavors or heat levels.
- **Change one variable per market:** price, bundle, sign, sample line or hero flavor (deep research, 2026). Log it so each weekend is a test.
- **Two price points with a clear step.** One maker priced 6.5 oz at $16 and 10 oz at $24; the bigger one sold best (operators report). For sauces, a 3-for price makes the bundle the default.
- **Sell the second unit.** "Two for $18, one to keep and one to give" beats a discount sign.
- **Merchandise sells the brand.** The hot sauce vendor sold T-shirts alongside bottles; carriers and branded items get photographed and shared.
- **Meet the neighbors.** Give nearby vendors a small sample and card at closing. One soap maker bartered bars for a honey vendor's gift baskets (operators report).
- **Hand over a reason to come back:** a card with "Bring this card to the store for 10% off your purchase of $30 or more" (suggested script, 22-pt comment), or a QR to reorder online where the license allows shipping.

## Events beyond the farmers market

- **Festivals and tournaments** carry premium prices: one observer saw about 20 people in line for $12 boba at a lacrosse tournament (operators report). Ask about exclusivity in your category.
- **Music festivals and food events** boosted a new mobile coffee bar's sales and social following (secondhand, r/smallbusiness).
- **Workplaces:** a sugar-scrub maker sold half a $50 batch on day one at a large office where coworkers already sold handmade goods, then $100 to $200 a month (operators report).
- **Designer and trade events:** an interior designer suggested branded popcorn favors for the furniture and tile showrooms that host designer events (suggestion, 69-pt comment). Corporate and event favors are a B2B channel for snack and sweets brands.
- **Trade shows (Expo West, Expo East, Fancy Food Show, PLMA):** costly. A mid-size co-packer spent $28,000 to attend Expo West and PLMA; commenters warned against crediting it all to one $150,000 contract and recommended multi-year tracking (r/manufacturing). For a small brand, walk a show first and book meetings in advance before buying a booth.

## In-store sampling

Measured lifts and costs are in [retail.md](retail.md#demos-and-velocity). The script is in [scripts.md](scripts.md). Rules:

- Demo only where the product is on that shelf that day, at a visible price.
- Hand the sample and say where it is and what it costs in the same sentence.
- Keep a tally: samples, units, and the 4 weeks before and after.
- A cookie brand famously ran paired taste tests with strangers in gas stations and grocery stores to choose its recipe (secondhand). Use paired tests before a reformulation, not just at launch.

## Cottage food: what the law allows decides the marketing

Cottage food laws let people sell certain low-risk foods made in a home kitchen. They vary by state and change often. Always read the current statute or the state agriculture or health department page, and the Institute for Justice "Baking Bad" survey, before advising.

Examples as reported by the Institute for Justice and secondary trackers (2025 to 2026):

| State | Cap and limits |
|---|---|
| Texas | $150,000 a year; in-person and online for pickup and delivery |
| California | Class A $75,000; Class B $150,000; microenterprise home kitchens $100,000 |
| Iowa | No cap for standard cottage food; home food processing establishments $50,000 |
| Delaware | No cap, but online sales banned (online advertising allowed) |
| Hawaii | Online sales and mail-order delivery banned |
| Colorado | New registration framework with a $150,000 gross cap from January 1, 2027 |

Trackers disagree on some states (North Dakota, Florida); third-party blogs are often wrong. Check the statute.

**What usually decides the plan:**

1. **Allowed products.** Baked goods without cream fillings, jams, candies and dry mixes are commonly allowed; refrigerated items, meat, low-acid canned foods and often acidified foods (hot sauce, pickles, salsa) are commonly excluded. Some states allow acidified foods: an Iowa sauce maker sells them under state rules and stays in contact with the inspections department (operators report). Know your pH and water activity either way.
2. **Allowed venues.** Direct to consumer only, or also to stores, cafes and restaurants?
3. **Online and shipping.** Many states allow online orders with local pickup or delivery but not shipping; few allow interstate shipping.
4. **Labels.** Most require a "made in a home kitchen" statement, ingredients and allergens. Use the state's exact wording.
5. **Sales cap.** Plan the move to a commissary kitchen or co-packer before you hit it. See [production.md](production.md).

**Marketing that fits cottage food:**

- **Weekly preorders plus a pickup window.** A home sourdough baker combined 10 to 20 preorders a week, 3 wholesale accounts (where allowed), a weekend farm stand and a few pop-ups a month; monthly income went from $385 in March to just over $2,000 in August, with $12 loaves, $15 inclusion loaves and $12 for four scones (operators report, r/sweatystartup 388-pt thread).
- **Occasional free giveaways** brought new customers to a home micro bakery (operators report).
- **Presell holidays.** Take orders from a preset menu before buying ingredients.
- **Bakery and cafe operations** (in-store, menu, delivery apps) belong to sibling `restaurant`.

**Never** advise shipping, wholesale or a product type the state law excludes, even if a competitor does it.
