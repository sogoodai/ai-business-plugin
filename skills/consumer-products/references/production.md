# Production: kitchens, co-packers and scale steps

Production choices set COGS, minimum runs, shelf life and which channels are legal. Marketing that outruns production loses accounts; production bought before demand burns cash. Validate demand at each step before committing to the next.

## The steps

| Step | Typical fit | Main constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Home kitchen under cottage food law | Markets, preorders, local pickup | Allowed products, sales cap, no wholesale or shipping in many states |
| Commissary or shared-use commercial kitchen | Local wholesale, farmers markets, early retail | Shift availability, your labor |
| Small-batch co-packer | Regional retail, DTC at volume | Run minimums, deposits, scheduling |
| Large co-packer or contract manufacturer | Distributor-scale retail | High MOQs, long lead times |
| Own facility | Only when co-packing blocks growth or quality | Capex, permits |

## Commissary kitchens: rent shifts, not buildings

A founder signed a three-month commissary lease at about $300 a month for one six-hour shift a week only after cafes asked for product, then expanded to three days a week at about $1,000 a month, using shared walk-ins, rack ovens and 80-quart mixers instead of buying equipment (operators report, r/Entrepreneur 1,885-pt thread).

Rules:

- Get buyer interest (samples accepted, orders promised) before signing.
- Prefer short terms that convert to month-to-month.
- Confirm the kitchen's license covers wholesale and the products you make.

## Co-packers

**What published minimums look like (2026 directory of manufacturer-published terms, compiled by a co-packer matching vendor):**

| Category | Minimum run |
|---|---|
| Sauces | 500 to 50,000 units |
| Beverages | two pallets to 20,000 bottles, or thousands of gallons |
| Supplements (one manufacturer) | 3,000 units |

Startup costs from the same source: SKU onboarding $500 to $2,500, setup or changeover $250 to $1,500+, deposits of 30 to 50% at booking. Planning data, not market averages.

**How to find a small-run maker first:** Native's founder started with an Etsy maker who accepted a first order of 100 deodorant sticks in one week, when manufacturers quoted 5,000 to 10,000 minimums and 4 to 6 months (operators report, secondhand). For food, look for small-batch co-packers, university food incubators and shared kitchens that offer contract production.

**Build the quote so it compares:**

```
per-unit cost = tolling or processing fee
              + ingredients (yours or theirs, with yield loss)
              + packaging (bottle, cap, label, case)
              + setup and changeover / units in run
              + lab testing per batch / units in run
              + storage and pick fees
              + freight to your warehouse or the distributor
```

**Questions for a co-packer:** minimum run per SKU and per flavor; whether flavors can share a run; who sources ingredients and packaging; who holds the scheduled process or process authority letter; batch records and lot coding; shelf-life testing; allergen controls; certifications buyers ask for (SQF or other GFSI for larger chains, organic, kosher, gluten-free); payment terms; who owns the formula. RFQ email in [scripts.md](scripts.md).

**Trick: when demand spikes, call the supplier on the box.** A novelty candy seller called the distributor printed on his first shipment's carton and ordered 6,000 units; unit cost fell from about $6 to $1.75 (operators report, 1,067-pt thread). Your packaging, ingredient and co-packer costs are negotiable at volume; ask for the next price break before you need it.

**Trick: finance the plant with a buyer's deposit.** A founder whose tea a chain wanted, but who lacked an approved facility, sold the chain 5,000 teapots first with a 50% advance of $50,000 and used the profit to build the facility (operators report, 1,910-pt thread). A supplement founder asked a quick-commerce chain to advance about 70% of costs before a reformulation for its 68 stores (operators report). Ask for deposits on large or custom orders.

## Acidified and low-acid foods (US)

Sauces, pickles, salsas and some dressings are often "acidified foods" under 21 CFR 114 when they are shelf-stable with a finished pH of 4.6 or below and water activity above 0.85. Refrigerated products are generally outside Part 114 (UGA extension).

- An operating supervisor completes an approved Better Process Control School course for acidified foods (university extension; Michigan State's self-paced version listed at $315).
- The facility registers with FDA and files each process (scheduled process) for acidified foods.
- A process authority reviews the recipe and process; keep the letter for buyers and co-packers.
- State rules can add requirements. Some cottage food laws exclude acidified foods; some allow them.

Know your product's pH and water activity before the first market. A commenter on a hot sauce maker's market post asked exactly this (operators report).

## Barcodes, lot codes, insurance

- **Barcodes:** buy from GS1 US. A single GTIN costs $30 one-time with no renewal; a 10-barcode company prefix is listed at $250 plus a $50 annual renewal (secondary sources, 2026; confirm on gs1us.org). Ten single GTINs cost $300, so the prefix is cheaper in year one if you will have 8+ products.
- **Never resold UPCs:** commenters on a 2017 thread warned that cheap UPC bundles can collide with another seller's registered codes; one reported Amazon removing a seller's entire store after the true GS1 owner proved ownership (operators report, 559-pt comment).
- **Lot codes on every unit.** Tabasco asked for the lot number before replacing a defective bottle (operators report); recalls and complaints need them.
- **Insurance:** most retailers and markets require product liability insurance with the retailer named as additional insured. Get the requirement in writing.

## Shelf life and packaging

- Test shelf life before retail. Distributors and Amazon reject short-dated product.
- Glass breaks. One brand's retail shipment arrived with broken bottles and stained labels; the owner replaced every damaged unit and added extra bottles (operators report). Use dividers and drop-test cases.
- Distinctive packaging carries price: a former hot sauce owner said an unusual cap let one brand charge $15 to $20 when most bottles were $5 to $8 (operators report). Commissioned label art earned awards for another maker at over $1,000 a label (operators report).
- Net quantity must be what the label says. A buyer weighed several 12 oz coffee bags from two roasters and found 10 oz; the thread drew 712 upvotes of distrust (operators report). Check fill weights on every run.

## Scale only on proof

| Before you commit to | Have |
|---|---|
| Commissary lease | Written interest from accounts, or a sold-out market season |
| First co-packer run | Repeat data, a channel that will absorb the run before its shelf life, cash for the deposit |
| Larger run for a chain | The PO, the free-fill and promo budget, and a reorder forecast based on sell-through |
| Own facility | Co-packer capacity or quality blocking growth for 2+ quarters |

Validate demand with images, preorders or small batches first. A candle maker launched with Photoshop images and next-day supplies and made product only after orders came in; a novelty candy seller bought inventory after about six orders in five days (operators report). For food, preorders and small commissary runs play the same role.
