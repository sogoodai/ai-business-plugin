# Pricing and offers

The 2026 industry grows on ticket, not visits: US salon revenue +3.48% with visits +0.98% and services +0.42% (PBA/KIM, September 2026). Price is doing the work, so price carefully and watch volume.

## Setting the price

- **Required hourly rate from real costs.** Put every cost in a spreadsheet, compute the hourly rate you must charge, redo it every year after taxes. Showing clients the logic takes the emotion out.
- **Revenue per sellable hour is the yardstick,** not price per service ([economics.md](economics.md)).
- **Check the market before lowering.** A Toronto nail tech charging CAD 90 to 180 for 2 to 3 hour sets asked peers after a complaint and kept her prices.
- **Long work earns at least what it replaces.** Six hours of fashion color must earn what six one-hour root touch-ups would. Charge corrections by the hour ($100 to $125 an hour is common among colorists; juniors charging $130 for 8 hours is the classic mistake).
- **Short and predictable can be the best business.** One 25-year colorist double-books root touch-ups and cuts, averages $170 to $200 an hour, and is booked out a year. Another keeps color-and-cut tickets under $180 and nothing over 2.25 hours, with a full book.
- **Price by contribution per chair hour.** Low-contribution services get repriced or an add-on, unless they are deliberate traffic drivers whose rebook and retail you measure.
- **Separate surcharges in the client profile** (long or thick hair, dematting, clarifying) so every stylist charges the same. A recurring 45 minutes of dematting inside a $35 blowout cost one stylist the next hour every three weeks.
- **Value beats discount** (Hormozi value equation, via coreyhaines31/marketingskills, MIT): value = dream outcome x likelihood / (time x effort). Most "lower the price" requests are really "raise proof" (portfolio of their hair type, reviews naming the service) or "lower effort" (online booking, lower-maintenance color) requests.

## Tiers that sell

- **Express menu under $100** for price-conscious clients (grey root retouch at $95 is many colorists' bread and butter).
- **Premium therapeutic or transformation tier:** one therapist charges $150 per 60 min and $225 per 90 min for deep therapeutic work with no spa extras and stays fully booked. Another bundles tip and every enhancement (hot stones, cupping, gua sha, aromatherapy) into the same price.
- **Longer sessions, lower hourly rate:** 60 min $100, 90 min $130, 120 min $175. One therapist grossed about $4,030 from 22 massages in five days.
- **Provider levels:** price by provider demand, not tenure. One salon expanded from 5 to 10 stylist levels to make smaller steps, and some separate internal promotions from client-facing price changes ([Salon Today](https://www.salontoday.com/articles/2026-salon-today-200-compensation-and-benefits), operator examples).
- **Paid upgrades instead of free pressure creep:** "Are we upgrading to deep tissue? No? Then we'll keep the pressure at a 6, and you can upgrade anytime." A deep-tissue surcharge also filters bookings that don't suit you.
- **Specialty styles priced as full sets every time** (anime lashes, large transformations), positioned for special occasions, with maintenance cost disclosed upfront.
- **Premium lash pricing rests on retention:** $250+ full sets are defended by 4 to 6 weeks of wear; big-city artists report $500 sets and $250 fills (operators report).
- **Tip-included pricing:** some solo owners build the tip into prices and advertise "we don't accept tips". Clients spend the same total and new ones book because of it.
- **Consultation as a priced menu item,** credited toward the service.

## Raising prices

Practitioners and the data disagree on the trigger. Rule: **review prices on a fixed date every year, but move them per provider according to utilization and waitlist, not across the board.**

- **Operators report:** a scheduled raise of about 5 to 8% every 18 to 20 months, or a fixed date each year, avoids painful catch-up jumps (one stylist left root touch-ups unchanged for 11 years). February works because clients book before tax refunds arrive.
- **Data:** 41% of Canadian beauty businesses raised prices in the prior year, planned raises averaging 8.1% (BeautyCouncil 2025); 72% of UK hairdressers had already raised (British Beauty Council 2026). In a Zenoti 2025 survey 64% of consumers said they had left a business they liked after a price rise, while 73% would pay more for personalized service and 43% would accept up to 10% more for it. Stated answers, not observed churn.
- **Decision rule:**
  - Provider below the category median utilization: fix demand, booking and positioning before price.
  - Provider near capacity with a waitlist while others have gaps: raise that provider or move them up a level. Price becomes a load-balancer.
  - Solo and booked out: raise. Departing legacy-price clients get replaced at current rates; those who nearly doubled prices report draining clients left and better ones stayed.
- **Break-even check:** you can lose p / (m + p) of visits before contribution falls (10% raise at 40% margin = 20%).
- **Roll out in waves** (adapted from coreyhaines31, MIT): new clients first, then a slice of existing clients, watch 8 to 12 weeks of rebooking and cancellations, then the rest. Grandfathering is a grace period, never forever.
- **Message with a date, a concrete reason, and a lower-priced option,** not an apology about inflation (template in [scripts.md](scripts.md)). Visits before the date stay at the old price.
- **Hold through complaints.** A $20 to $25 men's cut increase survived an 80-year-old regular's complaint with "When you go to the grocery store your groceries cost more? Mine do too." He rebooked. That line relied on years of banter; use the neutral template with clients you don't know well.
- **One-time grace:** honor the old price for that day's visit, then offer a lower-priced stylist if cost is still the issue. Most stay.
- **Explain with empathy:** "Would you want to work at a job for years where the only way to earn more was to work more hours?"

## Offers and discounts

- **Never percentage-off as the default.** Give a fast add-on instead: scrub, brow wax, sample, polish change. Discount retail, not services. Discount-seekers churn faster (ProfitWell data via coreyhaines31, attributed).
- **Offer test order:** no-discount proof, then a creditable consult or deposit, then an included add-on, then cash off.
- **Honest scarcity only:** real capacity ("two new-client spots in March"), real seasonal dates (prom, wedding season, before summer for laser), a real price-increase date. No resetting countdowns or fake "only 3 left".
- **Launch discounts are a content phase with an end date.** One esthetician discounted 50 to 80% to film content, then stopped; the next month she had 11 clients, $917 revenue and $717 profit.
- **Friend and family rates:** written end date, or restricted to quiet slots or once a month. An undated 50% friend rate lasted 18 months with no tips. Expire private supporter booking links.
- **Model calls** at minimum product cost, artist's choice of style, agreed upfront.
- **Off-peak packages** instead of blanket discounts: a bundle valid Tuesday to Thursday only, a limited count, or a themed off-peak day; same-morning flash openings to stories and the short-notice list (Brazil salon pack, MIT, uncited).
- **Groupon-style deals** bring one-time bargain hunters; if you run one, stack it with a reason to rebook at full price, and never stack it on top of a first-visit discount by default (one lash salon took a $100 set to $57).
- **Marketplace first visits** already cost 20 to 35%; adding a discount on top can make the first visit negative. Run the math in [economics.md](economics.md).

## Retail and add-ons

- **Retail is shrinking in units:** September 2026 professional retail revenue -1.32%, units -7.86%, average price +7.10% (PBA/KIM). Track attach rate and units, not dollars.
- **No robust retail norm exists.** Operators use 13% retail-to-service as a coaching trigger and 17% for higher commission (Salon Today); Vagaro's 12 to 15% is a target.
- **Recommend during the service,** while the client sees the result, and run retail and rebooking at the same checkout moment.
- **Retail that helps rebooking:** take-home shampoo and conditioner included in keratin pricing as a "maintenance bonus"; "fill it for 5" refills of a 2 oz bottle.
- **Add-ons surfaced at booking, in reminders, at POS and in the provider app** reached 25 to 30% attachment in one salon group (vendor case).
- **Never discount high-margin add-ons** clients would buy at full price.
- **Weigh color.** At about $0.20 a gram, eyeballed mixes leak margin and break formula consistency.

## Private practice math (massage and solo)

- Start with 2 or 3 regulars and rent a room by the hour or day before taking a full-time space.
- Offer spa clients private sessions below the spa price (for example $100 cash versus $120 plus tip) while earning more per session. Check your employment contract first.
- Validate local rates and realistic weekly bookings first: one private therapist's good week is 10 booked hours at $80 to $100.
- Never present hands-on hours as your whole workweek; a client who computed a therapist's "earnings" never returned. "As many as I can get, some weeks are busier than others."
- Delay big equipment: a $25,000 Hydrafacial machine with $1,500 monthly payments left one owner unpaid six months in. Choose product lines without opening minimums (often $1,000+).
- Certifications for marketing: require evidence of client gain first ($1,000+ and 100+ hours typical, $7,000 for one lymphatic course). Book a session with the practitioner whose technique you want and learn from them.
